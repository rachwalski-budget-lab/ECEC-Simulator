\section{Purpose}

The simulator reads one file for each geography. The file holds six rows, one
for each care type, and each row holds the annual hours of child care. This memo
gives the source of those numbers.

The build covers five geographies: Kentucky (KY), New Jersey (NJ), New York
(NY), New York City (NYC) and Virginia (VA). Every source is linked where it is
first used.

\subsection*{Why the build exists}

The simulator must know the mix of child care in each state, and the survey that
measures that mix cannot supply it.
\href{https://www.icpsr.umich.edu/web/ICPSR/studies/37941}{NSECE} does not record
the state of a family. It reports the Census region and nothing below it, so no
filter recovers a state from it.

I therefore build the mix from the administrative records of each state, and I
use NSECE only for the care that no administrative record shows. Two rules
follow from that, and they hold across every step below.

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.45em}
\item The build works at the state level. County data enters twice and only
      twice: step 1 sums counties to get the population, and the NYC build
      selects five of them, because NYC is a sub-state geography and cannot be
      defined any other way. No state-level geography uses a county anywhere, so
      the build needs no county crosswalk and no county name matching.
\item The build prefers a state measurement to a national one. It uses a
      national figure only where the state publishes nothing, and it says so
      each time it does.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{The position of NYC}

NYC is a geography of its own here, and every step below gives its figures. It
is also inside NY, and the NY figures include it. The city appears twice for two reasons.
The New York City Department of Health and Mental Hygiene regulates its centers,
not the state agency that regulates everywhere else. The city is also asked
about on its own.

The two builds do not agree, and that is deliberate. Each one takes its own
control total in step 2, and step 5 calibrates each one separately. The five
boroughs therefore come out differently in the two builds. Read one
or the other. Do not add them together.

\section{The build}

\subsection*{Step 1. Count the children}

I read the
\href{https://www2.census.gov/programs-surveys/popest/datasets/2010-2019/counties/asrh/cc-est2019-alldata.csv}{Census
population estimates} for 2019, keep children of age 0 to 4, and add the
counties together for each geography.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Children age 0 to 4, 2019}
\begin{tabular}{L{1.6cm}rr}
\toprule
& \textbf{Counties} & \textbf{Children 0--4, 2019} \\
\midrule
KY  & 120 &   272,610 \\
NJ  &  21 &   514,690 \\
NY  &  62 & 1,127,001 \\
NYC &   5 &   523,718 \\
VA  & 133 &   505,477 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

NYC uses five counties from the same file: Bronx 100,199; Kings 182,926; New
York 76,144; Queens 137,095; Richmond 27,354. The city holds 46 percent of the
children in NY.

The build stops at age 4 for three reasons.

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.45em}
\item The simulator stops there. Its ACS processing keeps children below age 5
      in seven places, so a wider demand file would anchor the model against
      households it never sees.
\item A universe of ages 0 to 5 raises each control total by 15 to 23 percent.
      That rise combines a population about 19 percent larger with a survey rate
      that moves, because five-year-olds use non-parental care less than younger
      children do. Most of them are in kindergarten, which the survey does not
      count as child care.
\item Census publishes no county estimate for age 5 on its own. The build can
      count a state at that age, but it could only estimate NYC, so the city
      would stop being counted and start being modeled.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Step 2. Find the children in non-parental care}

I read the
\href{https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/nsch/data/datasets.html}{NSCH
household survey} for 2019. I keep the children of age 0 to 4, weight each child
by the survey child weight, and take the share in any non-parental care. That
share times the population from step 1 is the control total. Every later count
is a share of it.

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.45em}
\item The control total is always a 2019 figure, because both of its inputs are
      2019 data. This is what allows the build to read a register published in
      2026 without ageing it backward: the register decides how care divides
      across types, and it never decides how much care there is.
\item NYC has no records of its own, which is why its row shows a dash. It takes
      the rate of NY, because NSCH publishes no geography below the state. Every
      difference between the city and the state therefore has to come from the
      registers in step 3, and it does.
\item This rate is the weakest number in the build. Each geography rests on
      about 120 families, which is a standard error of five to six percentage
      points on a figure that multiplies every later count. Carried through to
      the output, that is 17 to 23 percent on the hours in every row. Sub-step
      2.1 explains why the build still uses a single survey year, and sub-step
      2.2 what it does with the error instead.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{2.1 Why the rate uses one survey year}

Pooling survey years would cut the sampling error on the non-parental rate by 40
to 47 percent. No other change available here would buy as much accuracy for as
little work. The build
does not pool, because every input has to sit on the simulator's 2019 base year.

A pool of 2018, 2019 and 2020 has its midpoint exactly at 2019, so it is the one pool
that passes the vintage test. It fails on content instead. NSCH collected its
2020 data through the pandemic, and the use of child care collapsed.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Non-parental care rate by survey year}
\begin{tabular}{L{1.6cm}rrr}
\toprule
& \textbf{2018} & \textbf{2019} & \textbf{2020} \\
\midrule
KY & 57.3\% & 59.4\% & 45.8\% \\
NJ & 58.3\% & 60.2\% & 42.6\% \\
NY & 64.7\% & 57.7\% & 62.1\% \\
VA & 61.7\% & 52.0\% & 46.7\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

All four rows come from
\href{https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/nsch/data/datasets.html}{NSCH}. The
fall in 2020 is a real change rather than sampling noise, and pooling it in would
pull every total down by several points. A pool of 2018 and 2019 alone avoids
that, but its midpoint falls in the middle of 2018 while every other input is 2019.

So the rate keeps its full sampling error. The memo reports that error rather
than trading it for a bias that nothing downstream could recover. The repository
holds all three survey years, so anyone can repeat the test.

\subsubsection*{2.2 What the build does with the error instead}

The rate keeps its sampling error, so the build reports it. Alongside each
output file it writes \texttt{<GEO>\_interval.csv}, which carries a low and a
high figure for every care type.

The bounds are the rate plus and minus 1.96 standard errors. The build is re-run
at each bound rather than the central estimate being scaled, because the
response is not proportional: program enrollment is a published count and holds
fixed at every rate, so the calibration absorbs the difference and each care
type moves by a different amount. Unpaid Center-Based does not move at all.

This interval covers sampling error in the non-parental rate and nothing else.
It says nothing about the borrowed fill rates, the register vintages or the
program overlap. Those are not quantified anywhere, and at least the first is
likely to be larger.

\subsection*{Step 3. Convert the registers into children}

Every state licenses child care providers and publishes a register, and every
register reports capacity rather than children. Three sub-steps convert one into
the other.

\subsubsection*{3.1 Remove the school-age capacity}

Each register mixes school-age capacity into its figures, and each one hides it
differently.

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.45em}
\item Kentucky's Division of Regulated Child Care publishes no bulk file, so the
      build reads
      \href{https://services7.arcgis.com/s3vGpGobX9nzlLH3/arcgis/rest/services/Providers_Licensed/FeatureServer}{Child
      Care Aware of America's republication} of its licensed and certified
      registers. Neither register says which providers are centers and which are
      homes, so the build applies the state licensing rule: a licensed home
      keeps 12 children or fewer. That gives 1,609 centers and 335 homes.
      The build drops a provider that serves school-age children only. A mixed age band
      scales by a ratio between 0.487 and 0.827. The build measures each ratio
      inside the register itself. It compares the mean capacity of a band
      against the same band without school-age children.
\item The New Jersey Department of Children and Families (DCF) publishes
      \href{https://data.nj.gov/resource/cru5-4rmm.csv}{its register of licensed
      centers}, with an age range for each one. The build drops the 1,035
      centers serving ages 6 to 13 only, and scales a mixed range by 0.820. That
      scale is also measured inside the register, from two band comparisons that
      give 0.8224 and 0.8183.
\item The New York Office of Children and Family Services (OCFS) publishes
      \href{https://data.ny.gov/resource/cb42-qumz.csv}{its register} with
      capacity broken out by age, which is the clean case. Infant plus toddler
      plus preschool gives the under-five capacity exactly, so the build applies
      no scale.
      Homes report a single total, but the register publishes their school-age
      capacity separately and the build subtracts it.
\item NYC draws on both agencies. Its centers come from the New York City
      Department of Health and Mental Hygiene (DOHMH), whose
      \href{https://data.cityofnewyork.us/resource/gy3q-4tzp.csv}{permit file}
      gives one capacity figure per permit and covers children below age 6 by
      construction. Its homes come from the OCFS register and take the same
      subtraction as NY.
\item The Virginia Department of Social Services (VDSS) writes the age range as
      text in
      \href{https://legacy.dss.virginia.gov/facility/search/cc2.cgi}{its
      register}. The build parses it into months. It drops any provider starting
      at age 5 or later, and scales a center reaching past age 6 by 0.60. VDSS
      appends a contact block to that field, and the build strips it before
      parsing. Sub-step 3.4 gives what that cost when it was missed. Read
      correctly, a span ending below age six holds 0.527 of one running to
      nearly thirteen in Child Day Centers. It holds 0.604 in Religious Exempt
      centers. The two pool to 0.540, so 0.60 is the conservative end of the
      measured range. Homes take no scale at all, because VA caps a family day
      home at twelve children whatever ages it serves.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{3.2 Move the register back to 2019}

Three of the registers are 2026 exports and have to be brought back to the base
year.

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.45em}
\item NY scales each provider type separately, against the OCFS series of
      provider counts and capacity by modality covering 2015 to 2025. Group
      family day care scales to 0.86 of its 2026 size, and OCFS centers to 0.93.
      The DOHMH centers that NY also carries scale at 1.00, as they do in the
      NYC build. Homes have closed in numbers that centers have not, so one
      blanket factor would distort the mix rather than correct it.
\item NYC takes its own factors from the same OCFS series, which publishes the
      city separately from the rest of the state. Group family day care scales
      to 0.84 in the city. City centers scale to 1.00, because the city register
      held almost the same capacity in 2019 as it does now.
\item VA scales by one statewide factor of 0.912. The build computes it from
      VDSS's
      \href{https://data.virginia.gov/dataset/child-care-data-matrix-virginia-s-estimated-child-care-capacity-demand-june-2020}{Child
      Care Data Matrix} for June 2020, as that report's child care slots divided
      by the current register's capacity. The denominator is cut to the six
      approval routes the 2020 column names in its own header, so that it counts
      the same universe as the numerator. Dividing by all ten routes put 4,597
      places into the denominator that the 2020 figure never counted, and gave
      0.901 instead.
\item KY and NJ need no correction, because their registers already sit at the
      base year. The Child Care Aware republication is a September 2018
      snapshot, and the DCF register is frozen at May 2019.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{3.3 Convert capacity into children}

Capacity is a legal maximum, so real enrollment is lower and something has to
bring it down.

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.45em}
\item KY needs no fill rate, because it measured its own enrollment. The
      \href{https://www.childcareawareky.org/research/}{2019 Kentucky Workforce
      Study} prints capacity and enrollment side by side in Table 20, giving 42
      children for each center and 9 for each home. This is the preferred
      method, and it is why 1,609 centers yield only 67,578 children.
\item NJ measured its own fill rate. The
      \href{https://www.childcarenj.gov/Resources/Reports}{2021 NJ Child Care
      Market Rate Survey} puts its centers at 67 percent full in its 2020
      column.
\item NY, NYC and VA have published no fill rate, so they borrow the national
      figures: 90 percent for a center, from the ACF report on
      \href{https://acf.gov/opre/report/center-based-early-care-and-education-providers-2012-and-2019-counts-and}{center-based
      providers}, and 60 percent for a home, from the ACF report on
      \href{https://acf.gov/opre/report/home-based-early-care-and-education-providers-2012-and-2019-counts-and-characteristics}{home-based
      providers}. Sub-step 3.5 gives the evidence that both are too high.
\item NY and NYC homes use 80 percent instead of 60. The 60 percent figure is
      itself a product of a fill rate and an under-five share, and the OCFS
      register already removed the school-age capacity in sub-step 3.1. Applying
      the full figure would remove school-age children a second time, so those
      homes take the fill rate alone.
\end{itemize}

NY appears twice in that table because two agencies regulate child care there.
OCFS licenses providers across all 62 counties, but it licenses no center inside

before calibration. Every count in Results is after calibration; every count in
the table above is before it.

\subsubsection*{3.4 A parse defect in the Virginia register}

VDSS writes the age range and a contact block into one field:

\begin{quote}
\small\texttt{1 month - 12 years 11 months VDSS Contact:}\\
\small\texttt{Tara K Martin: (804) 588-2312}
\end{quote}

The build reads the upper bound as the text after the last hyphen. In a string like that one, the
last hyphen falls inside the telephone number. The upper bound therefore became
\texttt{2312}. That text holds neither ``year'' nor ``month'', so it parsed to
zero months.

A provider whose upper bound reads zero looks like a provider that serves no
school-age children, so the build kept 100 percent of its capacity. The rows
affected mostly run from one month to twelve years and eleven months, which is
the most mixed span the register holds.

Two things follow from this, and both matter more than the numbers.

The calibration concealed the defect. Step 5 forces Virginia's
formal care to the regional share whatever the register says. The extra 19,235
places therefore went into a more extreme scale factor, not into an obviously
wrong total. What looked like Virginia having an unusually complete
register was partly a parse failure.

The scale itself survived the fix. The 0.60 share was first measured on the
uncleaned strings and happened to land in the right place. Measured on the
cleaned field it is 0.527 for Child Day Centers and 0.604 for Religious Exempt
centers, so 0.60 remains defensible. The value did not change. What changed is
that it now rests on a field the build reads correctly.

The build now stops if any Virginia age range parses to an upper bound of zero
months. The same failure cannot return silently if VDSS changes the field.

\subsubsection*{3.5 Why the borrowed fill rates are too high}

Two states have published their own figures, and both point the same way.

KY measured its centers at 57 percent full in 2019, against the 90 percent that
NY, NYC and VA borrow. VA measured a related quantity in 2022, asking 1,968
providers what they can actually serve and comparing the answer to their license:
centers reported 73 percent of their authorized capacity and homes 91 percent,
with 121,260 licensed places in that sample against 89,374 children the providers
said they could take. That study is
\href{https://edworkingpapers.com/ai24-983}{EdWorkingPaper 24-983}.

The build uses neither figure as a rate. KY's applies to KY. VA's is from 2022
and measures capacity rather than enrollment, so it would duplicate a correction
the fill rate already makes. What both establish is the direction and rough size
of the error: licensed capacity overstates real supply by a quarter to a third,
and the national fill rate does not remove enough of it.

New York cannot be settled from its own survey, and that is now checked rather
than assumed. The
\href{https://ocfs.ny.gov/programs/childcare/}{2019 OCFS market rate survey}
publishes eleven tables. Every one of them reports rates, percentiles or
response rates. Its questionnaire does ask each provider how many children are
enrolled full-time and part-time in each age band, but no table reports the
answer. The fill rate NY would need exists in that survey's returns and not in
its output.

\setlength{\itemsep}{0.45em}
\item Pre-K comes from the school-level records of the NCES
      \href{https://nces.ed.gov/ccd/Data/zip/ccd_sch_052_1819_l_1a_091019.zip}{Common
      Core of Data} for 2018--19, counted school by school.
\item Head Start comes from the NIEER
      \href{https://nieer.org/state-preschool-yearbook-2019}{State of Preschool
      2019}, and Early Head Start from the NIEER
      \href{https://nieer.org/sites/default/files/2023-10/hs_fullreport.pdf}{report
      on Head Start}. Both are labelled ages 3 and 4, and birth to three, so
      every figure here sits inside the 0 to 4 universe. The two NIEER
      publications disagree slightly on Head Start in Kentucky: the yearbook
      gives 12,307 and the Head Start report 12,163. The build uses the yearbook
      figure for consistency with the other four geographies.
\item NYC takes its Head Start figure as a share of NY, using the ACF
      \href{https://s3foa.s3.us-east-1.amazonaws.com/HS_Service_Locations.csv}{service
      location file}: 7,194 funded places sit in the five boroughs against
      20,285 in the state, so the city takes 35 percent of the state level.
\item The build subtracts these children from the licensed center count rather
      than adding them, because a program center that holds a state license was
      already counted in step 3. It subtracts only the share that really is in
      the register, which sub-step 4.1 measures.
\item A published count does not move in step 5. The build treats it as a
      census, so the calibration falls entirely on the capacity-derived counts.
\item This row is an upper limit everywhere. Head Start and state pre-K overlap
      in an unknown number of children, and no source publishes the size of that
      overlap.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{4.1 How much of a program is already in the register}

A program site that holds a state license sits inside the capacity step 3
converted, so its children would be counted twice unless they are subtracted. A
site the register never lists does not, and subtracting it removes children who
were never counted in the first place. Until 18 September 2026 the build
subtracted everything, which assumed the first case always holds.

I measured it, for all three programs. I matched every open Head Start service
location in the federal file to the register. I did the same for every public
school that reports pre-K enrollment. The match uses ZIP plus street number plus
street name. It also uses coordinates within 25 metres, where both sides carry
them. Head Start is weighted by funded places, and pre-K by enrolled children. School addresses come from
\href{https://nces.ed.gov/programs/edge/data/EDGE_GEOCODE_PUBLICSCH_1819.zip}{NCES
EDGE} and pre-K counts from CCD school by school; those counts sum to exactly the
figures in step 4, which confirms where those constants came from.

Pre-K overlaps less than Head Start everywhere, which is what the licensing rules

it runs the largest school-based pre-K program of the five.

Three caveats.

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.45em}
\item A shared address is not proof that the same children were counted twice. A
      licensed provider at a school address can be a before-school or
      after-school program rather than the pre-K classroom. The match sets an
      upper limit on the duplication. It does not confirm it child by child.
\item The federal files are 2026 exports and the registers are not, so a site
      that has since closed or moved fails to match. Every figure is therefore a lower
      limit, and KY's is the least reliable because its register is from 2018.
\item VA can now be matched, but only across 58 percent of its register, so its
      figures are a floor rather than a measurement. Sub-step 4.2 gives what
      that allows and why the borrowed mean is kept.
\end{itemize}

VDSS publishes no address in the register the build reads, so Virginia was the
one geography whose overlap could not be measured. Its
\href{https://legacy.dss.virginia.gov/facility/search/cc2.cgi}{search tool} does
publish one. I harvested 3,183 addresses by locality and joined them to the
register on facility id, where 99.96 percent of harvested rows join.

\subsection*{Step 5. Calibrate the total to the survey}

A register describes the relative distribution across care types well and the
total badly: it puts far more children in formal care than any household survey
finds. The build corrects the total with one factor and leaves the distribution
alone.

\begin{enumerate}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item Take the share of non-parental care that is formal.
      \href{https://www.icpsr.umich.edu/web/ICPSR/studies/37941}{NSECE} gives it
      by Census region: 56.5 percent in the Northeast and 53.2 percent in the
      South.
\item Multiply that share by the control total from step 2 to get the target.
\item Hold the program counts from step 4 fixed.
\item Scale every other count by one factor until formal care reaches the target.
\end{enumerate}

The build computes each factor from the two totals, so nothing here is chosen by
hand. This table is the primary check on everything above it, and it reads as
follows.

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.45em}
\item Every factor is below one, which is the direction the evidence predicts.
      Capacity is a ceiling and the borrowed fill rates are too high, so a
      register should always overstate formal care.
\item NJ, NY and NYC sit between 0.86 and 0.90, close together despite three
      different agencies keeping their registers -- DCF, OCFS and DOHMH -- and
      despite two of the three borrowing their fill rates while the third
      measured its own.
\item KY falls least, to 0.78, and it is the only state that measured how full
      its own centers are: 57 percent against the 90 percent that NY and VA
      borrow. A state using its own fill rate needs less correction than one
      that borrowed a figure known to be too high, which is the ordering here.
\item VA falls furthest, to 0.59. The VDSS register is the most complete of the
      four, listing unlicensed and voluntarily registered family homes that no
      other state here records, and it also borrows the national fill rate. Both raise its count of
      formal care.
\item The spread has narrowed twice as assumptions were replaced by
      measurements: 0.62 to 1.58 when every program child was subtracted, 0.67
      to 1.17 once Head Start was measured, and 0.59 to 0.90 now that pre-K is
      measured too. An earlier run put three factors near 1.00, which looked
      reassuring but was an artifact of subtracting too much.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Step 6. Complete the rows}

\subsubsection*{6.1 Use no price split}

An earlier version of this build divided center care into low-priced and
high-priced. Nothing in the state data supports that division. A state publishes
one price. Every state therefore fell wholly on one side of the national
boundary. A care type left with no children then looks unmeasured rather than
genuinely absent.

Center care is now a single row. It carries 1,686 hours for each child each year,
which is the child-weighted mean across the two price bands that
\href{https://www.icpsr.umich.edu/web/ICPSR/studies/37941}{NSECE} distinguishes
nationally.

\subsubsection*{6.2 Calculate the care that no register records}

Three of the six types appear in no register anywhere: unpaid relatives, nannies
and informal arrangements. Unpaid relative care is not a business, files no
return, and leaves no administrative trace of any kind.

The build subtracts all counted care from the control total. It treats the
remainder as this care, and divides that remainder three ways on the national
pattern from
\href{https://www.icpsr.umich.edu/web/ICPSR/studies/37941}{NSECE}. The remainder
runs 44 to 47 percent of non-parental care across the five geographies, against
47 percent nationally in the same survey.

\subsubsection*{6.3 Convert children into hours}

Each care type carries a measured hours figure from
\href{https://www.icpsr.umich.edu/web/ICPSR/studies/37941}{NSECE}: the
child-weighted mean weekly hours reported for the child's primary arrangement,
multiplied by 50 weeks. The figures run from about 1,440 hours a year for program center care to about
2,120 for unpaid home care. The hours column is therefore not proportional to
the children column.

\section{Limits}

\begin{enumerate}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.45em}
\item The build cannot see unpaid relative care, which is the largest informal
      category nationally. That figure is a remainder rather than a measurement,
      and no administrative source would fix it. Only a restricted-access survey
      file with a state identifier would.

\item Three of the six rows carry no state information. Other Paid, Other Unpaid
      and Unpaid Home-Based are a national ratio applied to a residual, and they
      are 44 to 47 percent of all care in the file. The state content lives in
      the other three rows, and really in the population, the survey rate and the
      ratio of center to home capacity.

\item Paid Home-Based is not comparable across states. NY shows 84,223 children
      and VA 4,525, and that gap is mostly licensing law rather than behavior:
      OCFS registers a home provider keeping three or more children, and the
      other states register only larger ones. NJ has no home register at all.

\item The mix carries the date of the register. The total is 2019 by
      construction, but how it divides across care types reflects whenever the
      state last published. Composition genuinely drifts, so a 2026 register
      shows too few family homes for 2019 even after the vintage correction in
      sub-step 3.2.

\item Unpaid Center-Based is an upper limit everywhere, because Head Start and
      state pre-K overlap by an unpublished amount and a program site may also
      hold a state license.

\item Only one of the soft inputs carries an interval. Sub-step 2.2 propagates
      the survey rate's sampling error, but the borrowed fill rates, the
      register vintages and the program overlap have no stated error at all,
      and they are probably larger. The interval understates the real
      uncertainty and should be read as a floor on it.

\item Step 5 forces an exact match to the survey. That is a decision rather than
      a finding. The alternative is to let the registers stand and report the
      disagreement instead of removing it.

\item The simulator still expects seven rows, with center care split by price,
      and it rejects a file with six. Bridging the two is one mechanical
      operation that divides the center row on a national share. The build keeps
      that operation outside the method, so that it never appears to measure a
      quantity it cannot measure.
\end{enumerate}
