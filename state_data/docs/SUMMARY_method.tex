\section{Purpose}

\begin{enumerate}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item The simulator reads one file for each geography.
\item The file has six rows. Each row is one care type.
\item Each row holds one number. The number is the annual hours of child care.
\item This memo gives the source of that number.
\end{enumerate}

Two rules apply to the whole build.

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item \textbf{The build works at the state level.} It reads county data one
      time, in step 1, and it adds the counties together. No later step uses a
      county.
\item \textbf{The build uses a state measurement first.} It uses a national
      figure only where the state publishes none.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Why the build exists}

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item The simulator must know the mix of child care in each state.
\item NSECE measures that mix. NSECE does not record the state of a family.
\item NSECE reports the Census region only.
\item Therefore I build the mix from the administrative records of each state.
\item I use NSECE only for the care that no administrative record shows.
\end{itemize}

\section{The build}

\subsection*{Step 1. Count the children}

\begin{enumerate}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item Read the Census population estimates for 2019.
\item Keep children of age 0 to 4.
\item Add the counties together for each geography.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{L{3.2cm}r}
\toprule
& \textbf{Children 0--4, 2019} \\
\midrule
Kentucky      &   272,610 \\
New Jersey    &   514,690 \\
New York      & 1,127,001 \\
New York City &   523,718 \\
Virginia      &   505,477 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The build uses ages 0 to 4 for three reasons.

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item The simulator uses the same ages. Its code keeps children below age 5 in
      seven places.
\item A universe of ages 0 to 5 increases each total by 15 to 23 percent.
\item Census publishes no county estimate for age 5 alone. The build can count
      a state at age 5, but it can only estimate New York City.
\end{itemize}

New York City holds 47 percent of the children in New York.

\subsection*{Step 2. Find the children in non-parental care}

\begin{enumerate}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item Read the NSCH household survey for 2019.
\item Keep the children of age 0 to 4. Weight each child by the child weight.
\item Calculate the share of children in non-parental care.
\item Multiply the children from step 1 by that share.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{L{3.2cm}rrrr}
\toprule
& \textbf{Children 0--4} & \textbf{Rate} & \textbf{In non-parental care} & \textbf{Records} \\
\midrule
Kentucky      &   272,610 & 59.4\% & 161,930 & 147 \\
New Jersey    &   514,690 & 60.2\% & 309,946 & 101 \\
New York      & 1,127,001 & 57.7\% & 649,829 & 112 \\
New York City &   523,718 & 57.7\% & 301,976 & 112 \\
Virginia      &   505,477 & 52.0\% & 263,000 & 121 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The product is the \textbf{control total}. Every later count is a share of it.

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item \textbf{The control total is always a 2019 figure.} Both inputs are 2019
      data.
\item This is why the build can use a register from 2026. The register sets the
      mix of care. The register never sets the total.
\item \textbf{New York City uses the rate of New York.} NSCH publishes no rate
      below the state. The registers supply all city differences.
\item \textbf{This rate is the weakest number in the build.} Each state has
      about 120 families. The standard error is about seven percentage points.
      Section 4 explains why the build does not pool more survey years.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Step 3. Convert the registers into children}

Each state licenses child care providers. Each state publishes a register. The
register gives capacity. The build needs children. Three operations convert one
into the other.

\textbf{Operation 1. Remove the school-age capacity.} Every register includes
it. Each register hides it differently.

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item \textbf{Kentucky} does not mark a provider as a center or a home. The
      build applies the state rule: a licensed home keeps 12 children or fewer.
      This gives 1,609 centers and 335 homes. The build drops a provider that
      serves school-age children only. The build scales a mixed age band by a
      ratio between 0.487 and 0.827. It measures each ratio inside the register.
\item \textbf{New Jersey} publishes an age range for each center. The build
      drops the 1,035 centers that serve ages 6 to 13 only. It scales a mixed
      range by 0.820.
\item \textbf{New York} publishes capacity by age. Infant plus toddler plus
      preschool gives the under-five capacity exactly. A home reports one total,
      but the register publishes its school-age capacity. The build subtracts
      it.
\item \textbf{Virginia} publishes the age range as text. The build reads the
      text and converts it to months. It drops a provider that starts at age 5
      or later. It scales a center that serves past age 6 by 0.60.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Operation 2. Move the register back to 2019.} Two registers are 2026
exports.

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item \textbf{New York} scales each provider type against the state capacity
      series for 2015 to 2025. Group family day care scales to 0.86 statewide
      and to 0.84 in the city. Centers did not change. One factor for all types
      would make the mix incorrect.
\item \textbf{Virginia} scales by one statewide factor of 0.90. The build
      calculates it against the state capacity report for June 2020.
\item \textbf{Kentucky and New Jersey} need no correction. The Kentucky register
      is a snapshot from September 2018. The New Jersey register is a snapshot
      from May 2019.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Operation 3. Convert capacity into children.} Capacity is the legal
maximum. The true enrollment is lower.

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item \textbf{Kentucky measured its own enrollment.} The build applies no fill
      rate there. The 2019 workforce study prints capacity and enrollment
      together: 42 children for each center and 9 for each home. This is the
      preferred method. It is why 1,609 centers give only 67,578 children.
\item \textbf{New Jersey measured its own fill rate.} Its centers are 67 percent
      full. The figure comes from the market rate survey of 2021.
\item \textbf{New York and Virginia publish no fill rate.} They use the national
      figures: 90 percent for a center and 60 percent for a home.
\item New York homes use 80 percent instead. Its register already removed the
      school-age capacity. The 60 percent figure would remove it a second time.
\end{itemize}

New York needs two registers.

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item New York has two regulators.
\item The state licenses no center inside New York City.
\item The city permits its own centers under Article 47 of the Health Code.
\item The state register therefore shows zero centers in the city. This is
      correct. It is not a failure of the build.
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item \textbf{The build subtracts these children from the center count.} It does
      not add them.
\item A Head Start center usually holds a state licence. Step 3 therefore
      counted its children already. An addition would count them two times.
\item \textbf{A published count does not move in step 5.} The build treats it as
      a census.
\item \textbf{This row is an upper limit in each state.} Head Start and state
      pre-K overlap. No source publishes the size of the overlap.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Step 5. Calibrate the total to the survey}

A register measures the relative distribution across care types correctly. A
register measures the total incorrectly. It shows too many children in formal
care.

\begin{enumerate}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item Take the share of non-parental care that is formal. NSECE gives this share
      for each Census region. It is a little more than one half.
\item Multiply that share by the control total from step 2. This gives the
      target.
\item Hold the program counts from step 4 constant.
\item Scale all other counts by one factor until the formal total reaches the
      target.
\end{enumerate}

All scaled counts move together. The relative distribution does not change. Only
the total changes.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{L{3.2cm}rrrr}
\toprule
& \textbf{Scale factor} & \textbf{Formal} & \textbf{Informal} & \textbf{Informal share} \\
\midrule
Kentucky      & 1.58 &  86,082 &  75,848 & 47\% \\
New Jersey    & 1.04 & 175,212 & 134,734 & 44\% \\
New York      & 1.03 & 367,348 & 282,481 & 44\% \\
New York City & 1.05 & 170,707 & 131,269 & 44\% \\
Virginia      & 0.62 & 139,811 & 123,189 & 47\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

This table is the primary check on the build. Read it as follows.

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item \textbf{New Jersey, New York and the city change very little.} Their
      register counts came within two percentage points of the survey before the
      build changed anything. Three separate state registers agree with one
      independent national survey. This is the strongest evidence that step 3 is
      correct.
\item \textbf{The build reduces Virginia by one third.} Its register is the most
      complete of the four. It lists unlicensed and voluntarily registered
      family homes. Other states do not record those providers. Virginia also
      uses the borrowed national fill rate.
\item \textbf{The build increases Kentucky by one half.} Kentucky is the only
      state that measured how full its centers are. The measurement is 57
      percent. New York and Virginia use 90 percent. The national figure is too
      high by more than one half. Kentucky moves up and Virginia moves down,
      which is the result that this difference predicts. The calibration
      therefore corrects a measured error.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Step 6. Complete the rows}

\textbf{The build uses no price split.}

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item An earlier version divided center care into low-priced and high-priced.
\item A state publishes one price. Each state therefore fell on one side of the
      national boundary.
\item A care type with no children then reads as unmeasured, not as absent.
\item Center care is now one row. It holds 1,686 hours for each child each year.
      This is the national mean across both price bands.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{The build calculates the care that no register records.}

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item Three of the six types appear in no register: unpaid relatives, nannies
      and informal arrangements.
\item Unpaid relative care is not a business. It files no return.
\item The build subtracts all counted care from the control total. The remainder
      is this care.
\item The build divides the remainder three ways on the national pattern.
\item The remainder is 44 to 47 percent of non-parental care. The national
      figure is 47 percent.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{The build converts children into hours.}

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item Each care type has a measured hours figure from NSECE.
\item The figure is the mean weekly hours of the primary arrangement, multiplied
      by 50 weeks.
\item The figures run from about 1,440 hours for program center care to about
      2,120 hours for unpaid home care.
\item The hours column is therefore not proportional to the children column.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Why the rate uses one survey year}

A pool of survey years reduces the sampling error by 40 to 47 percent. The build
does not pool. Every input must match the 2019 base year of the simulator.

\begin{enumerate}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item A pool of 2018, 2019 and 2020 centres exactly on 2019. This pool passes
      the vintage test.
\item This pool fails on content. The survey collected the 2020 data during the
      pandemic. The use of child care fell.
\item A pool of 2018 and 2019 centres on the middle of 2018. Every other input
      is 2019.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{L{3.2cm}rrr}
\toprule
& \textbf{2018} & \textbf{2019} & \textbf{2020} \\
\midrule
Kentucky   & 57.3\% & 59.4\% & 45.8\% \\
New Jersey & 58.3\% & 60.2\% & 42.6\% \\
New York   & 64.7\% & 57.7\% & 62.1\% \\
Virginia   & 61.7\% & 52.0\% & 46.7\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The fall in 2020 is a real change. It is not sampling noise. A pool would
decrease each total by several percentage points. The rate therefore keeps its
full sampling error. The repository holds both extra survey years, so another
person can repeat this test.

\subsection*{Why the borrowed fill rates are too high}

Two states published their own figures. Both point in the same direction.

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item \textbf{Kentucky} measured its centers at 57 percent full in 2019. New
      York and Virginia use 90 percent.
\item \textbf{Virginia} measured a related quantity in 2022. It asked 1,968
      providers what they can serve, and compared the answer to their licence.
      Centers reported 73 percent of their licensed capacity. Homes reported 91
      percent. The sample holds 121,260 licensed places against 89,374 children.
\end{itemize}

The build uses neither figure as a rate.

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item The Kentucky figure applies to Kentucky.
\item The Virginia figure is from 2022. It measures capacity, not enrollment. It
      would duplicate the correction that the fill rate already makes.
\item Both figures show the direction and the size of the error. Licensed
      capacity is too high by one quarter to one third. The national fill rate
      does not remove enough of it.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limits}

\begin{enumerate}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.4em}
\item \textbf{The build cannot see unpaid relative care.} This is the largest
      informal category. The figure is a remainder, not a measurement. No
      administrative source would correct it. Only a restricted survey file
      would.

\item \textbf{The mix carries the date of the register.} The total is 2019 by
      construction. The division across care types comes from the last
      publication of the state. The mix changes over time. A register from 2026
      shows too few family homes for 2019.

\item \textbf{Unpaid Center-Based is an upper limit in each state.} Head Start
      and state pre-K overlap. A program site can also hold a state licence.

\item \textbf{Step 5 forces an exact match to the survey.} This is a decision,
      not a result. The alternative is to report the disagreement between the
      register and the survey.

\item \textbf{The simulator expects seven rows.} It divides center care by
      price, and it rejects a file with six rows. The bridge between the two is
      one mechanical operation. It divides the center row on a national share.
      The build keeps this operation outside the method, so that the build does
      not appear to measure a quantity that it cannot measure.
\end{enumerate}
