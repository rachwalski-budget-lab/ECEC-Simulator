\section{Purpose}

\begin{enumerate}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item The simulator reads one file for each geography.
\item The file has six rows. Each row is one care type.
\item Each row holds one number. The number is the annual hours of child care.
\item This memo gives the source of that number.
\end{enumerate}

The build covers five geographies: Kentucky (KY), New Jersey (NJ), New York
(NY), New York City (NYC) and Virginia (VA).

Each number in this memo carries a tag such as [S1]. Section 5.3 lists the
sources and gives the address of each one.

Two rules apply to the whole build.

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item The build works at the state level. It reads county data one time, in step
      1, and it adds the counties together. No later step uses a county.
\item The build uses a state measurement first. It uses a national figure only
      where the state publishes none.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Why the build exists}

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item The simulator must know the mix of child care in each state.
\item NSECE measures that mix [S5]. NSECE does not record the state of a family.
\item NSECE reports the Census region only.
\item Therefore I build the mix from the administrative records of each state.
\item I use NSECE only for the care that no administrative record shows.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{The position of NYC}

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item NYC is a geography of its own in this build. Every step below gives its
      figures.
\item NYC is also inside NY. The NY figures include it.
\item The two builds do not agree. Each build uses its own control total from
      step 2, and step 5 calibrates each one separately.
\item Use one build or the other build. Do not add them together.
\end{itemize}

\section{The build}

\subsection*{Step 1. Count the children}

\begin{enumerate}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item Read the Census population estimates for 2019 [S1].
\item Keep children of age 0 to 4.
\item Add the counties together for each geography.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{L{1.6cm}rr}
\toprule
& \textbf{Counties} & \textbf{Children 0--4, 2019} \\
\midrule
KY  & 120 &   272,610 \\
NJ  &  21 &   514,690 \\
NY  &  62 & 1,127,001 \\
NYC &   5 &   523,718 \\
VA  & 133 &   505,477 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

NYC uses five counties [S1]: Bronx 100,199; Kings 182,926; New York 76,144;
Queens 137,095; Richmond 27,354. NYC holds 47 percent of the children in NY.

The build uses ages 0 to 4 for three reasons.

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item The simulator uses the same ages. Its code keeps children below age 5 in
      seven places [S23].
\item A universe of ages 0 to 5 increases each total by 15 to 23 percent [S1].
\item Census publishes no county estimate for age 5 alone [S1]. The build can
      count a state at age 5, but it can only estimate NYC.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Step 2. Find the children in non-parental care}

\begin{enumerate}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item Read the NSCH household survey for 2019 [S2].
\item Keep the children of age 0 to 4. Weight each child by the child weight.
\item Calculate the share of children in non-parental care.
\item Multiply the children from step 1 by that share.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{L{1.6cm}rrrr}
\toprule
& \textbf{Children 0--4} & \textbf{Rate} & \textbf{In non-parental care} & \textbf{Records} \\
\midrule
KY  &   272,610 & 59.4\% & 161,930 & 147 \\
NJ  &   514,690 & 60.2\% & 309,946 & 101 \\
NY  & 1,127,001 & 57.7\% & 649,829 & 112 \\
NYC &   523,718 & 57.7\% & 301,976 & 112 \\
VA  &   505,477 & 52.0\% & 263,000 & 121 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

All rates and record counts in this table come from NSCH 2019 [S2]. The product
is the control total. Every later count is a share of it.

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item The control total is always a 2019 figure, because both inputs are 2019
      data [S1, S2].
\item This is why the build can use a register from 2026. The register sets the
      mix of care. The register never sets the total.
\item NYC uses the rate of NY [S2]. NSCH publishes no rate below the state. The
      registers supply all NYC differences.
\item This rate is the weakest number in the build. Each geography has about 120
      families [S2]. The standard error is about seven percentage points.
      Section 5.1 explains why the build does not pool more survey years.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Step 3. Convert the registers into children}

Each state licenses child care providers and publishes a register. The register
gives capacity. The build needs children. Three sub-steps convert one into the
other.

\subsubsection*{3.1 Remove the school-age capacity}

Every register includes school-age capacity. Each register hides it differently.

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item KY does not mark a provider as a center or a home [S6, S7]. The build
      applies the state rule: a licensed home keeps 12 children or fewer. This
      gives 1,609 centers and 335 homes. The build drops a provider that serves
      school-age children only. The build scales a mixed age band by a ratio
      between 0.487 and 0.827. It measures each ratio inside the register [S6].
\item NJ publishes an age range for each center [S9]. The build drops the 1,035
      centers that serve ages 6 to 13 only. It scales a mixed range by 0.820.
\item NY publishes capacity by age [S12]. Infant plus toddler plus preschool
      gives the under-five capacity exactly. A home reports one total, but the
      register publishes its school-age capacity. The build subtracts it.
\item NYC uses two registers. Its centers come from the city register [S14],
      which gives one capacity figure for each permit and covers children below
      age 6 by construction. Its homes come from the state register [S12] and
      take the same subtraction as NY.
\item VA publishes the age range as text [S19]. The build reads the text and
      converts it to months. It drops a provider that starts at age 5 or later.
      It scales a center that serves past age 6 by 0.60.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{3.2 Move the register back to 2019}

Three registers are 2026 exports.

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item NY scales each provider type against the state capacity series for 2015 to
      2025 [S13]. Group family day care scales to 0.86. Centers scale to 0.93.
      One factor for all types would make the mix incorrect.
\item NYC takes its own factors from the same series [S13], which publishes the
      city separately. Group family day care scales to 0.84 in NYC. City centers
      scale to 1.00.
\item VA scales by one statewide factor of 0.90. The build calculates it from
      the state capacity report for June 2020 [S20].
\item KY and NJ need no correction. The KY register is a snapshot from September
      2018 [S6]. The NJ register is a snapshot from May 2019 [S9].
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{3.3 Convert capacity into children}

Capacity is the legal maximum. The true enrollment is lower.

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item KY measured its own enrollment [S8], so the build applies no fill rate
      there. The 2019 workforce study prints capacity and enrollment together:
      42 children for each center and 9 for each home. This is the preferred
      method. It is why 1,609 centers give only 67,578 children.
\item NJ measured its own fill rate [S10]. Its centers are 67 percent full.
\item NY, NYC and VA publish no fill rate. They use the national figures: 90
      percent for a center [S3] and 60 percent for a home [S4].
\item NY and NYC homes use 80 percent instead [S4]. The state register already
      removed the school-age capacity in sub-step 3.1. The 60 percent figure
      would remove it a second time.
\end{itemize}

S19]. Children come from the conversion in sub-step 3.3.

NY needs two registers.

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item NY has two regulators.
\item The state licenses no center inside NYC [S12].
\item NYC permits its own centers under Article 47 of the Health Code [S14].
\item The state register therefore shows zero centers in NYC. This is correct.
      It is not a failure of the build.
\item NYC uses the city register for centers and the state register for homes.
      The state licenses home providers in NYC.
\end{itemize}

NJ has no register of home providers. Its 1,987 children come from a survey
instead [S10]: 1,396 children below age 5 across 913 responding providers,
raised to the 1,254 registered providers.

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item Pre-K comes from the school-level records of NCES for 2018--19 [S15].
\item Head Start comes from NIEER for 2018--19 [S16]. Early Head Start comes

\item NYC takes its Head Start figure as a share of NY [S18]: 7,194 funded

      counted its children already. An addition would count them two times.
\item A published count does not move in step 5. The build treats it as a
      census.
\item This row is an upper limit in each geography. Head Start and state pre-K
      overlap. No source publishes the size of the overlap.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Step 5. Calibrate the total to the survey}

A register measures the relative distribution across care types correctly. A
register measures the total incorrectly. It shows too many children in formal
care.

\begin{enumerate}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item Take the share of non-parental care that is formal. NSECE gives this share
      for each Census region [S5]. It is 56.5 percent in the Northeast and 53.2
      percent in the South.
\item Multiply that share by the control total from step 2. This gives the
      target.
\item Hold the program counts from step 4 constant.
\item Scale all other counts by one factor until the formal total reaches the
      target.
\end{enumerate}

All scaled counts move together. The relative distribution does not change. Only
the total changes.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{L{1.6cm}rrrr}
\toprule
& \textbf{Scale factor} & \textbf{Formal} & \textbf{Informal} & \textbf{Informal share} \\
\midrule
KY  & 1.58 &  86,082 &  75,848 & 47\% \\
NJ  & 1.04 & 175,212 & 134,734 & 44\% \\
NY  & 1.03 & 367,348 & 282,481 & 44\% \\
NYC & 1.05 & 170,707 & 131,269 & 44\% \\
VA  & 0.62 & 139,811 & 123,189 & 47\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

This table is the primary check on the build. Read it as follows.

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item NJ, NY and NYC change very little. Their register counts came within two
      percentage points of the survey before the build changed anything. Three
      separate registers [S9, S12, S14] agree with one independent national
      survey [S5]. This is the strongest evidence that step 3 is correct.
\item The build reduces VA by one third. Its register is the most complete of
      the four [S19]. It lists unlicensed and voluntarily registered family
      homes. The other states do not record those providers. VA also uses the
      borrowed national fill rate [S3, S4].
\item The build increases KY by one half, and this is the informative result. KY
      is the only state that measured how full its centers are. The measurement
      is 57 percent [S8]. NY and VA use 90 percent [S3]. The national figure is
      too high by more than one half. KY moves up and VA moves down, which is
      the result that this difference predicts. The calibration therefore
      corrects a measured error.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Step 6. Complete the rows}

\subsubsection*{6.1 Use no price split}

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item An earlier version divided center care into low-priced and high-priced.
\item A state publishes one price [S21]. Each state therefore fell on one side
      of the national boundary.
\item A care type with no children then reads as unmeasured, not as absent.
\item Center care is now one row. It holds 1,686 hours for each child each year.
      This is the national mean across both price bands [S5].
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{6.2 Calculate the care that no register records}

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item Three of the six types appear in no register: unpaid relatives, nannies
      and informal arrangements.
\item Unpaid relative care is not a business. It files no return.
\item The build subtracts all counted care from the control total. The remainder
      is this care.
\item The build divides the remainder three ways on the national pattern [S5].
\item The remainder is 44 to 47 percent of non-parental care. The national
      figure is 47 percent [S5].
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{6.3 Convert children into hours}

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item Each care type has a measured hours figure from NSECE [S5].
\item The figure is the mean weekly hours of the primary arrangement, multiplied
      by 50 weeks.
\item The figures run from about 1,440 hours for program center care to about
      2,120 hours for unpaid home care [S5].
\item The hours column is therefore not proportional to the children column.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{5.1 Why the rate uses one survey year}

A pool of survey years reduces the sampling error by 40 to 47 percent [S2]. The
build does not pool. Every input must match the 2019 base year of the simulator.

\begin{enumerate}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item A pool of 2018, 2019 and 2020 centres exactly on 2019. This pool passes
      the vintage test.
\item This pool fails on content. The survey collected the 2020 data during the
      pandemic [S2]. The use of child care fell.
\item A pool of 2018 and 2019 centres on the middle of 2018. Every other input
      is 2019.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{L{1.6cm}rrr}
\toprule
& \textbf{2018} & \textbf{2019} & \textbf{2020} \\
\midrule
KY & 57.3\% & 59.4\% & 45.8\% \\
NJ & 58.3\% & 60.2\% & 42.6\% \\
NY & 64.7\% & 57.7\% & 62.1\% \\
VA & 61.7\% & 52.0\% & 46.7\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

All figures in this table come from NSCH [S2]. The fall in 2020 is a real
change. It is not sampling noise. A pool would decrease each total by several
percentage points. The rate therefore keeps its full sampling error. The
repository holds all three survey years, so another person can repeat this test.

\subsection*{5.2 Why the borrowed fill rates are too high}

Two states published their own figures. Both point in the same direction.

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item KY measured its centers at 57 percent full in 2019 [S8]. NY, NYC and VA
      use 90 percent [S3].
\item VA measured a related quantity in 2022 [S21]. It asked 1,968 providers
      what they can serve, and compared the answer to their licence. Centers
      reported 73 percent of their licensed capacity. Homes reported 91 percent.
      The sample holds 121,260 licensed places against 89,374 children.
\end{itemize}

The build uses neither figure as a rate.

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
\item The KY figure applies to KY.
\item The VA figure is from 2022. It measures capacity, not enrollment. It would
      duplicate the correction that the fill rate already makes.
\item Both figures show the direction and the size of the error. Licensed
      capacity is too high by one quarter to one third. The national fill rate
      does not remove enough of it.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limits}

\begin{enumerate}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.4em}
\item The build cannot see unpaid relative care. This is the largest informal
      category [S5]. The figure is a remainder, not a measurement. No
      administrative source would correct it. Only a restricted survey file
      would.

\item The mix carries the date of the register. The total is 2019 by
      construction. The division across care types comes from the last
      publication of the state. The mix changes over time. A register from 2026
      shows too few family homes for 2019.

\item Unpaid Center-Based is an upper limit in each geography. Head Start and
      state pre-K overlap [S16, S17]. A program site can also hold a state
      licence.

\item Step 5 forces an exact match to the survey. This is a decision, not a
      result. The alternative is to report the disagreement between the register
      and the survey.

\item The simulator expects seven rows. It divides center care by price, and it
      rejects a file with six rows [S23]. The bridge between the two is one
      mechanical operation. It divides the center row on a national share. The
      build keeps this operation outside the method, so that the build does not
      appear to measure a quantity that it cannot measure.
\end{enumerate}
